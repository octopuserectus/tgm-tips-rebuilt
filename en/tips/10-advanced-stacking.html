To keep a good stack reliably in 20G, where your range of movement is restricted, it is not enough just to “fit pieces into whatever dents you are given”; what matters is to “actively build a stack that is easy to stack on.”
(The placements from Chapter 6 that build up the fifth column are the prime example.)

Keep a stack that lets the I piece reach the right edge

→
Stand it upright in columns 8–9 and the stack as a whole becomes “a gentle slope with moderate steps.”
At low gravity there would be nothing wrong with that, but in 20G you can no longer get the I piece into the right edge.
So stop at columns 7–8 instead: by keeping the two right-hand columns low, you keep a stack that lets the I piece reach the right edge.
→
Here too, leaving the two right-hand columns open lets you take the I piece all the way to the right edge.
The side hole can be followed up by sliding in a J, or with a T twist.
In 20G, where “if there is a step, the piece always drops to the bottom of it instantly,” slides like these are very easy to use, and once you are used to them you can slip pieces in with very quick inputs.
Learn to tell at a glance which piece the shape is waiting for.
Slides like these, where the piece stays lying flat, are also effective in time attack.
A pattern that builds a shape you can follow up with a twist.
T and Z pieces come in handy.
“Hooking” placements like these are really handy.
Once you include the ones that take several pieces to follow up, there are far too many placements of this kind to show them all.

For the right-hand side of the playfield, the points are:
・make side holes that open to the right
・leave the right-hand two or three columns open
If a side hole opens to the right, you can nearly always follow it up with a slide or a twist, and if a two-column width is open, any piece can pass through. The idea of “stopping partway” works well in real play.

Ideally you would know beforehand which piece can follow up, but to begin with that is not strictly necessary: go by feel, make side holes, just try all sorts of placements, and find the patterns that suit you.

Taking the I piece to the left

To take the I piece to the left you need a space of at least 3×2.
The O is usually a nuisance, but here it is useful as a base that creates the two rows of height.
The input is “left 1.”
With the synchro introduced in Chapter 7, a dent in column 2 can be jumped over.

Also, leaving the left three columns open means making column 4 high, which means stacking one column to the left of column 5.
That is why the “left 1” input becomes so useful.
If column 4 is one row higher than column 2, you just need to add one row. Standing a J upright “on the spot” (その場立て, sonoba-tate) works well.
If column 4 is one row lower than column 2, you just need to add three rows. A T with “left 1” works well.

Waiting for a particular shape

Patterns that wait for an S at the left edge.
Notice also that column 4 is already high once the first piece is down, so the S can be taken to the left straight away.
Stabbing an L-shaped piece in upright.
A surface that goes up and down one cell at a time is commonly called a “castle” (城, shiro); stab a J or an L into it and you have a wait for a T twist. (Left figure.)
It is easy to come up with if you keep “lay a Z or an S flat in a 2×1 dent” in mind. (Right figure.) The pattern of pointing a J to the left and waiting for a Z slide is extremely effective.
A 3×2 dent is a chance for the “somersault” (でんぐり返し, denguri-gaeshi).
At the left edge, learn the input “S, left 2” as part of the same set.
Better still if, depending on how the pieces are coming, you can alternate it with stopping at left 1 and waiting for an L (or O → J).

Compromise placement

In 20G, where you stack column 5 high, it follows that the closer a hole is to column 5, the harder it is to dig back out.
So even when you are forced to make a hole, the idea of keeping it as far from column 5 as possible becomes important.

→
A shape where you have no choice but to make a hole.
It is tempting to think vaguely, “let’s fill in from the lower rows,” but putting the Z in leaves a hole in column 5, and recovery is difficult.
What matters is the vertical way of thinking: “fill the columns nearest column 5 first.”
→
A slightly less obvious pattern: putting a Z where an S looks as if it would fit.
Note that if you try to put an S in, it will not rotate.
→
A pattern where you slide in a J or an L, which fills three rows of height.
→
Stab the J in upright and you clear two lines, but if you let the line clear in front of your nose lead you astray, you end up with a hole in column 5.
When the stack is low there is a case for writing those lines off, but when you are about to start recovering, a hole in column 5 is fatal. Whatever else you do, fill column 5.

If an I piece comes first and you cannot place it on the right, take it to the left even if that makes a hole. Defending column 5 to the last is worth that much.
