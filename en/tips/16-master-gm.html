As I said in Chapter 14, the M-roll is an extremely difficult stage in which each piece becomes invisible the instant it locks. Your first time, you may well top out without clearing a single line.
Beating it calls for a different way of thinking from everything so far. Basically you have to memorize the stack, but you don’t need to memorize all of it—only as much as it takes to hold out for one minute.
If you’re someone who can simply remember it all, so much the better, but for most people that’s probably impossible, so you need ways of making the stack as easy to memorize and as hard to forget as you can.

Hit the wall, fit into a dent

The way to beat it is to concentrate your thinking on memorizing the stack as far as you possibly can, and to spend no unnecessary attention on anything else.

Take the controls, for example. Fiddly inputs like double taps, or placements where the piece has to be stopped at the edge of a cliff, pull your attention their way, and the rest of the stack slips out of your memory.
In the M-roll you stack without manual locking, to buy as much time as possible, and the worry then is “tampering with the past.” If you’re busy minding your timing so as not to tamper with the past, memorizing the stack goes out the window.
To spare yourself that worry, place pieces so that they can’t move any further—fit them into a dent, or push them against a wall. In other words, it becomes important to choose placements that put DAS first.

Even when a piece doesn’t move sideways at all, deliberately pushing the stick toward the wall anyway, to confirm that the stack is there, helps you not to forget it.

Choices that put the surface first

Heavy use of DAS generally leaves the middle sunken, and you end up with holes in the center that are hard to recover from. In the M-roll, though, to hang on to your memory even a little longer you should use DAS heavily, as I said above, and if that creates a hole, just stack right over it without a second thought. Basically, you don’t think about recovery.
Out of habit you may find yourself wondering where you left what kind of hole, but it’s important to cut those thoughts off cleanly and concentrate on keeping track of the surface.

For what it’s worth, I picture a white line on the playfield on the screen. Some people apparently play while picturing in their head the playfield as it would normally look.
→
At times it also becomes important to say, “I could still place this without making a hole, but I’ll make one on purpose for the sake of a smoother stack.”
To my mind, the M-roll is decided not by stretching out “how long you can remember the stack perfectly” but by how well you can cut your losses like this and lay a fresh foundation. Once you’re in a situation where “I have no choice but to make a hole,” it’s too late. The point is having the decisiveness to make the hole yourself “before that.”

You will almost never be able to remember everything from start to finish. I think it’s about right to go in expecting to rebuild your foundation like this at least three times in order to clear it.

Remember how many columns are open on the left

Something that helps when you’re rebuilding your foundation, among other times: whenever you place a piece in the middle, also take note of “how many columns does this leave open on the left?”
For example, any piece other than the O, laid flat where it spawns, leaves three columns open on the left. Placed at left 1, it leaves two columns open, and so on.
This doesn’t necessarily hold unless the middle is high, but it’s well worth remembering.
