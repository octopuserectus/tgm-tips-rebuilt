Getting across dents

A basic example: using IRS to turn the two-column-wide edge downward and get across a one-cell dent.
It may be a good idea to get into the habit of spawning the J and the L in this orientation.
You can also use a step in the stack to get across a dent.
“The J triple rotation”
You turn the wide edge downward so that the piece does not drop into the dents.
In most cases the first rotation has to be done with IRS.
A pattern like the one on the right is somewhat harder; you have to get through the second rotation quickly.
It helps to get a feel for deliberately charging DAS late.

When you must not use IRS

The S and the L are shapes that go to the left easily, but you must not use IRS when they are going to land on column 4.
Don’t leave it until “the moment” arrives: learn to have “if an S or an L comes, no IRS” in mind from the point where the wait takes shape.
Both of these are “left, left, rotate.”
Dawdle and the piece will lock, so learn to enter the input quickly and with confidence, without following the piece’s movement with your eyes.

Taking the I piece to the edge

Getting the I piece into the right edge takes a 2×2 space.
So if the stack is shaped like a staircase, the I piece can be put in at the right edge.
As long as the 2×2 space is there and the piece gets all the way to the edge, the other bumps don’t matter.
Learn to toss it in on the right with confidence.
Getting it into the left edge, on the other hand, takes a 3×2 space.
So with a staircase shape on the left, the I piece will not go in.
Examples of the input “sideways, charge sideways, rotate.” The trick is to flick the stick twice in quick succession and keep it held the second time.
It is easy when the rotation makes the piece fall (resetting the lock delay), but tight when it doesn’t. Waiting until you see the piece appear is too late, so you need to judge the timing of its appearance.
So, with a shape like this, can the I piece be put in at the left edge?
Put the fact that “at 20G a piece always falls to the bottom of any step” together with the way the I piece rotates, and it looks as though it would get caught in column 3.
But in this case, if you

“take it to the edge lying flat with left charged, and rotate without letting go”

you can get it into column 1.
The principle is explained below.

Synchro

This means performing several inputs together within a single frame.
Chapter 1 explained that the frame is the smallest unit of screen drawing; within each frame, the checks are known to be made in the order

rotation → sideways movement → falling

If you do both a rotation and a sideways move within one frame, sideways movement in mid-air becomes possible even at 20G.
When people say “synchro” they generally mean advanced techniques like the ones introduced in Chapter 12, but for the sake of explanation I am treating it here more broadly, as a “phenomenon.”

Let’s look at it using the example above.
From this position, press a button while still holding the left charge.
As explained in Chapter 1, a charged DAS is the same as entering a sideways input on every frame, so this counts as “sideways and rotation entered at the same time.”
And then…
First comes the rotation check (if the piece can rotate, it rotates).
This happens inside the computer and is not drawn on screen.
Next comes the sideways movement check (if the piece can move sideways, it moves).
Nothing is drawn on screen at this point either.
After that comes the falling check (at 20G, the piece falls all the way down), and the result is drawn on screen.
Since DAS is charged, the piece drops into column 1 on the next frame.
Of course, if you return the stick to neutral after reaching the left edge and then rotate, the piece stands upright in the third column from the left as usual, so use whichever suits the situation.

The back hip circle

This one, too, looks as if it would get stuck in column 3, but with the input “charge left, rotate” you can easily prop it upright.
A slightly harder pattern, in which you take the piece over with a right charge and then re-charge to the left.
This kind of thing is perhaps what people most often mean when they say “back hip circle.”
Be careful: without enough charge the piece slips down, and if you rotate late it just locks where it is.

Incidentally, if you can enter sideways and rotate simultaneously, there is no need to charge. It is worth having a go when the stack is one where it would do no harm even if the piece did slip down.

Valley crossing

Even without the advanced triple-rotation crossing introduced in Chapter 9, there are cases where you can use a step to get across a two-column-wide gap.
It is often combined with the back hip circle described above.
Learn to read the stack and do it with confidence.
