Earning the GM grade

Once you can reach level 999, aim for the highest grade, GM.
The minimum requirement for GM is to reach level 999 within 13:30 with an S9 grade (120,000 points or more).
There seem to be a few other conditions as well, such as S4 at level 500, but if you stack quickly and cleanly it should not be all that hard.

You might think speed and cleanliness pull against each other, but remember that being clean is what makes you fast.
If the stack is clean, it is easy to find where to place a piece and easy to grasp the shape you will have afterwards. The inputs also tend to be simpler.

Once you can get GM, take on time attack and aim to reach level 999 in under 10 minutes.
Working hard at manual locking alone should get you to around 11 minutes, but from there on you will be shaving off time a few tenths of a second at a time.
You drop more than 700 pieces between the start and level 999, so getting 0.1 seconds faster per piece makes you more than a minute faster overall.

Movement optimization 1

In 20G, in a situation like this, how do you stand the S upright while leaving the rightmost column open?
At low gravity you could rotate it upright, take it to the right edge with a right charge and then bring it back one, but in 20G it would fall in.
Taking it there by tapping right one column at a time needs four taps, which takes time.
This is where the following input becomes extremely effective:
Carry the piece to the edge with a right charge in the orientation it spawned in, and once it reaches the edge, return the stick to neutral and rotate.
The same input can be put to use with the T, L and I.
Do it slowly and surely at first. Once you are used to it, it will snap into place in one flowing motion.
Early on, while gravity is low, you do not even need to return the stick to neutral:
once the piece reaches the edge, you can hold down to soft drop and rotate as it falls.

These are patterns that take the piece to the left.
An application of the above:
carry the piece to the edge with DAS, then bring it back one and rotate while soft-dropping.
If you can do this with confidence even when the stack is high, reaching level 500 in under 5 minutes is no mere dream.

With the methods above, you should be able to get by with no more than two sideways inputs during the early, low-gravity part of the game.
If you find yourself doing something like right-right-right, or right charge then left-left, that input is not optimal.

Clearing lines together

When you clear lines, ARE is 0.7 seconds longer than when you do not, so naturally the fewer line clears you make, the faster your time.
Taking the loss from a Tetris as zero, each clear costs you 0.18 seconds for a triple, 0.35 seconds for a double, and 0.53 seconds for a single.

“I can recover right away, so I’ll take a single with the I piece” is something to think twice about in time attack.
You want to develop it into a double, a triple or, if possible, a Tetris.

That said, never force it. Especially at the stage where you are aiming to reach level 999 somewhere between the low 10-minute range and the high 9s,
keeping a constant manual-lock rhythm matters more than straining for multi-line clears.
For example, in the TGM movie published on Arika’s official site, you can see that although the play is built around Tetrises, a fair number of smaller clears are mixed in as well. Even so, by making full use of movement optimization and keeping the manual-lock rhythm constant, it turns in the extremely fast time of 9:19.
Develop your clears only as far as you can without hesitating.

What do you do when an S comes and there is no suitable place for it?
In 20G a slide is one option, but early on it costs time. (That said, once gravity has picked up to some extent there is no great need to go out of your way to avoid it.)

If you stand it upright and stab it into the well, you make a single and, on top of that, have to clear lines one more time before you have recovered.
And because you are standing a piece upright, the stack afterwards tends to get rough.
What I recommend instead is laying it flat, as if putting a lid on.
That way you can recover with a single line clear.

Better still, stack an O and then an L on top and you are set up to recover with a Tetris. Learn them as a set.
That L placement is handy for adding just one more cell of lid on a narrow foothold.
With a Z, stand it upright like this.
Put a T on top and it develops into a triple recovery.

Stacking high early on

The soft drop you get from holding down is 1G, which is pretty fast, but long falling distances still add up to a considerable loss of time, so the standard practice is to stack high early on and keep the time pieces spend in the air as short as possible.
If you are aiming for a GM in the 9-minute range, learn to stack up to around 16 rows and still do your movement optimization without charging late.

Even when you have stacked high, as long as you leave the two right-hand columns open you can always escape by skimming, so topping out because of a bad piece sequence practically never happens.
It is a contest of how short you can make the falling distance, so the smart move is probably to give the left edge priority and build it right up to the top.

Also, a soft drop from the very top of the playfield to the very bottom takes 0.3 seconds, so given that the loss from a triple is 0.18 seconds,
a triple high up is worth more than a Tetris at the very bottom.
So if you stack in a way that leaves you ready for either a Tetris or a triple, you can stack more boldly and should also lose less time to hesitation.
→
When you end up with a hole low down, it is often better not to think about recovering right away and just stack on top of it regardless.
Early on, whoever lands their pieces highest wins, so recovery can wait until gravity has picked up.

“It isn’t a mistake until you make a compromise clear.”

One thing to watch out for: as the stack gets higher, the time from a piece spawning to it locking gets shorter, so inevitably the pitch at which pieces appear gets faster.
If you carry on with the same feel as right after the start, your reactions gradually fall behind, and you may well find your DAS charge coming late and the piece dropping one column short.

Movement optimization 2

A quick double rotation that flips the piece, done by using the C button as well.
In some cases you can fire them off one after another with the help of wall kicks.
In this case, if you hold C for IRS and then input A and down the moment the piece appears, you can lock at the fastest possible timing.

Incidentally, I said in Chapter 7 that processing within a frame happens in the order rotation → sideways movement → falling; the check for locking by manual lock happens after all of that.
So in this case the button for the second rotation and down on the stick can be simultaneous (within the same frame).
The quick double rotation can also be used on the left side for the T twist that kicks off the wall.
Here, if you keep your ring finger (I use my little finger, perhaps because my hands are small) bent slightly more than your index finger and just let the weight of your wrist drop them onto the buttons (so that C goes down, then A), it is easy to do, and because you can think of it as a single motion, you can move on to the next thought right away.
Rather than “A twice = A, C,” it is easier to think of it as “to flip a piece, hammer the buttons at both ends.”
After standing a Z upright with one tap left, the J goes “right, B, left.”
Using the wall kick lets you avoid a double tap (two separate taps sideways), and it is a powerful weapon in time attack and in the advanced stages of TAP as well.

Note that the piece will not wall kick unless you first shift it one column sideways.
It is a fine point, but because the order is rotation → sideways movement → falling, even if you press the button after the stick, if both land in the same frame the rotation is evaluated first, and the piece can end up not rotating.
Again, until you are used to it I recommend doing it slowly and surely.
Examples where a wall kick turns a double tap into a single sideways input.
Here too the second rotation and the manual lock can be simultaneous, so you can lock at much the same timing as inputs like “DAS → down” or “sideways → down.”
I think the strength of optimization using wall kicks lies not only in the input time it saves in itself, but also in the fact that you can keep your manual-lock rhythm constant. Right after a double tap, it takes time to get your rhythm back.
You will not get many chances to use it, but the twist that lays a piece face down can also be put to work for optimization.

DAS pre-charge

A “DAS pre-charge” means that, instead of working out the placement and then starting to charge, you charge DAS to one side or the other for the time being and think about where to place the piece while you do.
That said, in TGM it is almost always used with a left charge.

The ideal in time attack is for the charge to be complete before the piece appears, and for the piece, once it appears and reaches the edge, to lock immediately without stopping for even a single frame; in practice it comes down to how close you can get to that.
In fact, the accumulation of tiny losses from charging late is a real nuisance in time attack.
You can review how you stacked as much as you like afterwards, but a split-second hesitation during play is not the kind of thing that is easy to look back on and fix.

With the DAS pre-charge, if you find a place for the piece on the left, the delay in charging is zero. Even if an input other than a left charge turns out to be needed and you have to start over, the loss is not that large.
Pick your moments by reading the state of the stack and the flow of the pieces, and it becomes more efficient still.
Ideally you use it for three or four pieces in a row, in which case the stick movement is a repetition of “left charge → down for an instant the moment the piece appears, then left charge → …”

Using the DAS pre-charge also helps you bring your manual-lock timing forward.
Pieces in TGM are fairly heavy (the DAS delay is long), so ordinarily it is not unusual to be unable to tell whether your charge was late or not late at all. That makes it hard to manual lock boldly at an early timing.
When a DAS pre-charge succeeds, however, you are playing in the certain knowledge that your charge was not late at all, so you can repeat the experiment of “that timing was OK, so next time I’ll try pressing down an instant earlier,” and finding the optimal timing becomes easier.

It has all sorts of benefits, but be aware that overusing it carries the risk of wrecking your stack.
