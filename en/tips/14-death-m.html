Death mode at a glance (TAP)

• Up to level 100 it is about on par with the 700s of Master mode, except that the DAS delay is slightly longer.
• The 100s are faster than the 800s of Master mode.
• The 200s are slower than the 900s of Master mode.
• The 300s are faster than the 900s of Master mode.
• The 400s are faster than the 300s.
• Level 500 onward is faster than the 400s.

• If your time is over 3:25 at level 500, the credits roll there and the game ends. This is called “torikan,” from the Japanese for “a counter stop, for now.”
• Passing level 500 earns the M grade, and reaching level 999 earns the GM grade.
• Carrying a manual lock over from piece to piece is impossible from the very start.

Carrying IRS over

From level 300 on ARE becomes extremely short, so it is not just that the game moves fast: the input timing for IRS becomes tight.
If you try to press the button early, the previous piece may not have locked yet and you end up with a pre-lock input.
To prevent this sort of thing, it is sometimes handy to exploit the fact that IRS can be carried over (if you keep holding the button you used to rotate one piece, it remains valid as the IRS input for the next piece).
That is, instead of deciding whether to press the button, you decide whether to release it. Releasing a button changes nothing about the piece, so there is no risk of a pre-lock input.
Since having a button held down becomes your basic stance, it feels odd until you get used to it, and there are also cases where the rotation direction is restricted and you cannot carry over smoothly, but I think it is a technique with a fair amount of practical value.

In the example in the figure:
• The moment the O shows up in NEXT you can tell that the stack is going to end up needing an I, so as soon as you notice, just press A (any button will do) and hold it.
• The L that follows is to be flipped over, so once it appears, release the A you are holding, press A once more and keep it held.
• For the S after that you can release A and press it yet again; personally I go A→B instead, because that flow matches how I picture the motion.
• If you hand over to each next piece like this with a button always held down, the IRS for the I piece is guaranteed to succeed.

Skimming and compromise placements with the DAS pre-charge

To beat 3:25 at level 500, you will struggle unless you pass level 300 within 2:05.
It would be another matter if you could keep manual locking after level 300 too, but once you factor in things like the time lost to level stops, you would ideally like to pass it within 2 minutes.
However, in the early part of Death mode the pieces feel heavier than in the late part of Master mode, and time is easily lost to charging DAS late.

This is where the DAS pre-charge introduced in Chapter 11 comes into its own. (I originally came up with it to get past a plateau in my times in the early part of Death.)
What is more, unlike TGM, where you have to keep getting Tetrises as far as possible, in Death all you need to do is get the pieces down fast, so you can charge to the right as well as to the left.
In particular, when you get a stack that slopes down to the right with the right two or three columns open, it is powerful if you can throw pieces in on the right one after another.
The key is how well you have absorbed the skimming patterns.
In TGM, to keep the stack clean, even after a DAS pre-charge you have to adapt and change your inputs as the situation demands, but in Death you can choose stacking options that put more priority on making use of the charge. It is even possible to use hardly any double taps at all.
Going further, there is also the approach of putting input speed and a flat stack first even at the cost of making holes. It is handy to learn ways of making holes that are easy to dig back out.
The trick is to lay the piece flat so that it covers the hole like a lid. Try out various patterns.

Carrying DAS over

Once you can reach the torikan consistently and are at the level of aiming for Death M, there are times when you are unsure whether to manual lock in the 300s.
Because ARE is extremely short, pressing down carelessly makes the game absurdly fast and your judgment cannot keep up, yet if you play without it the 300s can feel pretty slow. And even so, if you are being careful to avoid pre-lock inputs, the losses from late charges pile up, and then one short stall at a level stop is enough to put the time out of reach.

For such times there is the method of carrying DAS over.
In essence it is the version of the DAS pre-charge without the manual lock, but its distinguishing feature is that you can keep the stick held sideways even when a piece is not going to move sideways, like the T and the I piece in the figure.
Naturally it works not only to the left but also when you send a batch of pieces to the right; the key point is when to switch direction. If you can reverse the stick the way it is done on the O in the figure, doubling back and running the piece into a wall, you get zero late charge with no risk of a pre-lock input.

It goes without saying, but having no late charges means the pieces come down at a correspondingly faster pace than usual, and in the 400s especially this demands very quick judgment, so doing it for too many pieces in a row is dangerous. To begin with, chain about two or three pieces and see how it goes.
