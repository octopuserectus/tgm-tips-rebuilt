Floor kicks for the I and T pieces

For the details, see KAN’s guide.
Now that the I piece moves more flexibly, you no longer have to worry much about “building a stack that lets the I piece travel all the way to the edge,” which makes it easier to keep up fast stacking. For that very reason, though, be careful: you can find yourself, before you know it, going faster than you can actually control. While the stack is reasonably clean you can keep on stacking, but once it falls apart there’s a danger of being swept away all at once.

Hold

On Hold, I think 613’s idea of “a two-way choice between NEXT and Hold” is extremely useful.
To become fluent with Hold, don’t wait until a situation “requires” it—just use it like crazy whenever you can. As the speed picks up, there are times when your head knows this is the moment to use it but you hesitate and your hand won’t move, so put in the sheer numbers until you can press the button with confidence.

Three next pieces

I think the single most important thing in Ti is to “look at all three next pieces.” Being able to see further ahead and change how you stack accordingly matters, of course, but the fact that a piece spends a long time on screen before it enters the playfield means a great deal in the world of ultra-high speed. Don’t overcomplicate it at first; just “looking” is enough. Something on the level of “having a glance at what pieces are coming up” is fine. The ability to think about them comes as you get used to it.

What matters is that this “ability to look at three next pieces” isn’t something you can stop thinking about once you’ve acquired it; you have to keep paying attention to it all the time. As you aim for faster speeds and higher grades, the urge to “stack as cleanly as possible” grows stronger, and because of it your attention drifts to the stack and your eyes drift downward. Before you know it you’re looking at only one next piece. Even looking at just one, the I piece’s movement and Hold described above bail you out, and in the short term you can stack passably well. So it’s hard to notice while you’re absorbed in play, but your stacking lacks stability, and this can be the reason you stop improving.
