Master mode at a glance (TAP)

・From level 0 to level 499 the game speeds up in much the same way as TGM.
・It likewise hits 20G at level 500, except that ARE, the line clear delay and the DAS delay all become slightly shorter.
・In the 600s the line clear delay becomes obviously shorter.
・In the 700s ARE and the line clear delay shorten, and the game moves along at quite a clip.
・In the 800s ARE and the line clear delay shorten further. Pieces come down at the same pace whether you clear lines or not.
・In the 900s the lock delay shortens. You can no longer carry the down input over from one piece to the next.

・The “sonic drop,” done by pressing up on the stick, is introduced. The piece reaches the bottom in an instant, but it only lands and does not lock, so you use it together with a manual lock.
・For the conditions for earning medals, see KJU’s lab.

The “gogogo” at high speed

In fast play, mainly from level 800 onward, it can happen that you press down to lock a piece, but that piece has already locked, so you lock the piece that came after it instead, and then yet another piece drops in and startles you. This is generally known as “to gogogo” (ゴゴゴる, gogogo-ru).
When the lock delay is short, as in the 900s, you can gogogo even without pressing down. It happens when “lock → next piece appears” comes “sooner than you thought.” When you know when the piece will spawn, you can cope reasonably well even if it is early, and even if you hesitate and your inputs come late, you are at least aware that “I was late because I was dithering.” But when a piece appears at a moment you did not expect, your body takes time to react, and that is time people are not normally conscious of. In other words, time really has passed but you have no sense of it, which creates the illusion that the piece “locked instantly.”

Key points for the 900s

Up through the 800s the lock delay stays the same, so as long as your reactions keep up, what you “can do” does not change. In the 900s, however, the lock delay is roughly halved, so what you can do starts to be limited. What is called for, then, is simple handling that does not take many inputs.

Cases like the ones in the figures, where you have to charge DAS in a situation that allows no lock delay reset, go without saying, but you should also avoid the two-tap sideways “tap-tap” (コンコン, konkon) as far as you can (unless the piece can drop down a step, which is another matter).

In short, the key is to think about placements giving priority to inputs such as DAS alone, one tap sideways, or dropping in place.
A section is short, so it is possible to scrape through on momentum alone, but if you are aiming higher still, work toward clearing it with “solid stacking” from as early on as you can.

The credit roll and grades

In TGM the credit roll after maxing out the level counter was something like a victory lap, but in TAP it is a full-fledged stage of the game to be conquered.
When you max out, every block in the playfield is cleared away once, and then pieces start falling again at the same speed as the 900s while the credits scroll.
Normally these pieces become invisible about 5 seconds after they lock. (They are still there.)
If you survive this to the end, about one minute, your entry on the ranking screen gets an orange underline; this is called a “full clear.”
If you top out partway through the credit roll you get a green underline, and this is called a “max-out clear.”

Furthermore, if you max out with “fast” and “skillful” play, the credit roll becomes a high-difficulty stage in which pieces turn invisible the instant they lock.
This is what is called the “M-roll” or the “invisible roll,” and as far as has been confirmed so far, if this credit roll appears and you top out partway through you always get the M grade, and if you survive to the end you always get the GM grade.
M has been confirmed with a green line, and GM with a green line and with an orange line, but apparently there are other grades as well.

Aiming for the M grade

The conditions for getting the M grade are to a large extent shrouded in mystery, and it is not unusual for a game that is extremely fast and cleanly stacked, one that top players are convinced “has to be an M,” not to get M.
The following is quoted from the Data Room.

・No M has been confirmed with a max-out time of 8:50 or over.
　(However, beyond a max-out time of 8:45 it is extremely unlikely.)

・M can be awarded even with level 500 at 5:02.
　(However, beyond 5:00 it is extremely unlikely.)
・M can be awarded even with level 100 at 1:04.
・M can be awarded even with level 200 at 2:02.
・M can be awarded even with a score of 208,939 (8:40.71).
　(However, it is somewhat unlikely under 230,000 and considerably unlikely under 220,000.)
・M can be awarded even with a bronze SK medal.
　(That was with heavy use of combos, however. Normally it is extremely unlikely without at least a silver SK medal.)
・M can be awarded even with grade 2 at level 500 and reaching S9 at the same moment as maxing out.
・M can be awarded even with S6 at level 900.
　(However, the targets are grade 1 at level 500, S4 at level 700 and S7 or better at level 900.)
・M can be awarded even without a single Tetris in the 900s.
・M can be awarded even without a single Tetris in the 800s.
・M can be awarded even without any manual locking from level 900 on.
・M can be awarded even without using up (sonic drop) at all.

・Level stops appear to have nothing to do with the M conditions.
・Medals appear to have no direct connection to the M conditions.

Once you are able to max out, aim first for maxing out within 8:45.

The target for the first half (passing level 500) is within 5 minutes. You can call this all but mandatory.
Try to have the silver SK lit by then as well. If the bronze SK lights up before level 200, that is a very good pace.
Do not let the time for any one section run far over a minute. (Around 1:05 is the limit?)
Since you have the up input, stacking high the way you do in TGM is not needed for cutting time, but there is also a theory that M is hard to get with the so-called “tenpai, instant Tetris” style (聴牌即テトリス, tenpai soku Tetris: taking the Tetris the moment you are ready for one), so I think it is better to maintain a certain height.

In the second half, especially from 700 on, a constant pace of manual locking is important. I believe that if you were racing along at full speed and then a mistake makes your speed drop abruptly, it costs you a lot of points.
So rather than the knife-edge timing of TGM, acquire a manual lock timing that leaves a certain safety margin.
From experience, I get the feeling that it is not good if the intervals between line clears open up too much. Even when you are building the stack up high, mix in doubles and triples here and there.

The Zangi-move

By using up, which “only lands the piece and does not lock it,” you can use slides and twists freely even in the early game when gravity is low.
And by including among your options placements that set up a wait for one of those, it also becomes possible to avoid tap-taps, which take time to input.
The Zangi-move makes the most of these properties of the up input to give you both time savings and comfortable handling.
The name comes from the special move command of a character in a certain famous fighting game, in which you whirl the stick around in a circle.

→
In the early game of TAP, the inputs for sliding a Z in on the right side are

“charge right, (once it reaches the right edge) up to sonic drop, left to slide, down to lock”

but what this comes down to is simply whirling the stick 270° around from right to down.
This is the most basic pattern of the Zangi-move.
You can also use it in situations other than those that call for a slide.
Since you stack leaving the one column on the right open to wait for the I piece, the opportunities to use this will inevitably be very numerous.
It is also effective on the left side when you are placing a piece one column away from the wall.

In this case the down input serves not only to lock but also as a soft drop, but for a fall of up to two or three rows or so there is no need to consciously hold down for longer; your usual feel is fine. (As you will find if you try it, however briefly you mean to press down, the piece inevitably falls about three rows anyway. That is exactly why using slides in the early game of TGM leads to a big loss.)
Patterns that combine it with rotation.
In some cases the timing of the rotation can be pretty loose, but━━
━━in cases like these it will not work unless you are careful.
Until you get the feel for it, it is safer to do it slowly.
This one is a move for placing near the center by turning the stick through 180°.
The up input can be buffered, so if you hold up during the previous piece’s ARE and whirl the stick around the instant the piece appears, you can move almost as if you were in 20G.
The Zangi-move also has the effect of reducing input errors.
In this case, for example, if you do “right, up, down” in the ordinary way, it can happen that right gets input unintentionally as you move the stick from up to down, and the piece ends up shifted farther than you wanted. We call this “side slip” (横ずれ, yoko-zure).
It is fine when the piece has settled into a dent at the moment of the sonic drop and cannot move, but take care when you have shifted it right and there is room for it to slip further right.

That said, depending on the person, it may on the contrary be the Zangi-move that is harder to use, because of a higher risk of failing to get the sideways input in.
It also varies considerably with the stick. Personally, on a Seimitsu stick (see below) I tend not to use the Zangi-move much.

About sticks

Sticks divide broadly into those made by Seimitsu and those made by Sanwa.
The photo on the left shows the plate, called a restrictor gate (“sub-guide”), attached to the underside of each stick; the stick’s shaft moves within this opening. Tetris uses only the 4 directions up, down, left and right, so diagonals cannot be input (in the case of the Seimitsu gate, the upper opening is the one being used).

When you whirl the stick around, the Sanwa is the one that moves smoothly in a ◇ shape, and the Seimitsu is the one that catches and grinds because of protrusions on the inside.

In my experience the Sanwa is easier to whirl, so the Zangi-move is easier on it. Even a Seimitsu becomes easier to whirl once it has seen a fair amount of use and the bumps have worn down. If it catches too much, you get the “failed sideways input” mentioned above.
With a Sanwa, however, perhaps because many of them have a long shaft and a large throw, it can happen that when you let go after pushing sideways to let the stick return to neutral, the force of the spring carries it straight through to an input on the opposite side. Concretely, you meant to input “right, right” quickly but what gets input is “right, left, right, left,” and the piece just shudders on the spot and locks. We call this “rebound” (反動, handō). I also feel that the “side slip” introduced above happens more easily with a Sanwa. Some of this can be solved by how you handle the stick, though.

A stick that moves in a □ or ○ shape when you whirl it is set to 8-way (diagonals can be input too). Fighting games and shooters are usually set to 8-way.
As for what happens if you play on an 8-way stick: when you push a diagonal, sideways is given priority. So when you meant to push straight down but were actually on a diagonal, it is judged to be a sideways input, and the piece makes a sideways move you did not intend.
The manual for the TAP board states explicitly, “Be sure to set the stick to 4-way,” so speak to the arcade staff and have them change it.
