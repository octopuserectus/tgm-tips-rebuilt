Overview

• The ARIKA logo appears at 44 seconds, stops in the middle at 48.5 seconds, and the roll is cleared at 54.5 seconds.

The basic shape is a wait for the I piece (a Tetris) at the right edge. As I touched on in the previous chapter, making small line clears frequently means the stack (its surface) changes at a dizzying pace, which makes it hard to memorize. Given the I piece’s mobility as well, I think you can say that stacking for Tetrises is actually the safer way.

Get the rhythm at the start

In the invisible roll, most of your thinking has to go into “memorizing.” So even a player who can manual-lock away furiously when the stack is visible may, if they’re not used to the work of memorizing, find their thinking can’t keep up and get “beaten by the speed”—which is why you need to be properly prepared for the speed. At the start in particular, the whole stack is wiped and the playfield is completely bare, so there are no lock delay resets at all; the distance to NEXT changes; the music changes; with that much change in your surroundings, I think you’ll be playing for a while without a grip on the rhythm. If at this point you’re so taken up with memorizing that you neglect to get hold of the rhythm, you have no future.
Before the roll begins, take a good look at the three next pieces and settle exactly where the first three pieces will go. Worrying about whether pieces “disappear” and turning your eyes to the piece that has just dropped is a no-no. For checking whether it disappeared, the corner of your eye really is enough. (For this, it might be good to practice stacking at the start of Shirase while looking down as little as possible.)

Rotation placement on the right

Basically you’ll mostly be placing pieces starting from the left, but I think a major point is to level columns 8 and 9 as early as possible and fix the stack in its shape of waiting for an I piece at the right edge. For one thing, “DAS right, then tap left” carries the risk that, unless you remember the stack on the right, the piece gets stuck at the right edge and won’t come back. Also, every piece other than the I leaves the rightmost column open with “right, right, right (without rotating),” but that’s dangerous unless you know the piece can get down the steps on the way.
So what I recommend is “rotation placement” of the S, T, L and I pieces (a term I’ve just made up; what you actually do is the same as the movement optimization in Chapter 11). The thing to note about this maneuver is that, as long as you can carry the piece to the edge (with the L, rotating it three times is more effective), a stack that stops it from rotating there practically never occurs in real play. It will always rotate. Put another way, you can place the piece without ever blocking the right edge. It would be a waste not to make good use of this. It’s quite common that “I don’t remember the stack on the right exactly, but I know I can carry this over to the right laid flat,” and it gives you more time to think ahead. Keeping your eyes on the three next pieces is, of course, the basic premise, but as a practical matter, in the invisible roll there are many times when you have to watch to make sure a piece moved the way you intended (returning your gaze right afterward, of course), and being able to skip that means a lot.
If you also learn the patterns “right, right” + standing the I piece upright, and “right” + a rotation placement that leaves the piece two columns wide, they prove their worth when you’re rebuilding your foundation and at other such times.

Don’t be stingy with the I piece

In the invisible roll, where “ease of memorizing” is a high-priority factor in how you stack, the idea of a two-way choice between NEXT and Hold that I introduced in Chapter 17 is a great help. I think you should use Hold more and more. In particular, leaving an I piece sitting in Hold is a waste. In the invisible roll you don’t often run short of I pieces; if anything, I suspect it’s more common for an oversupply of them to get in your way. So I think it’s better to go in with the attitude that if there’s somewhere to put an I piece, you pull out the one you’re holding without hesitation. (It’s a different story when you’ve built the stack fairly high and the Tetris well is deep.)

Stay wary that the line might not clear

If you place a piece expecting a line to clear and it doesn’t, because there was a hole somewhere you didn’t expect, that’s a recipe for panic. In particular, when you have leveled the stack with compromise placements → stacked cleanly on top of that → put an I piece in at the right edge, don’t let yourself have already forgotten the compromise placements and end up thinking “but I stacked so cleanly—why?” When something you thought wouldn’t clear does clear, you can cope fairly easily just by delaying your next input for a moment; but when you expect a clear and it doesn’t happen, the next piece comes down sooner than you anticipated, and that is when a piece locks on you before you know it.
