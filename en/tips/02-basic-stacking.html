Moderately bumpy, gently contoured overall

You end up with lots of deep wells, can’t fill them all, and top out…
To keep that from happening, fill gaps in before they turn into trouble.

Three kinds of piece fit a gap two deep. That is three of the seven pieces, so you can expect one roughly half the time and there is no need to be too scared of such gaps; but once you have several of them things get dangerous, so you want them filled early.
When the wall on one side is three high, only two kinds of piece fit. Make filling these a priority.
At a depth of three or more, you are waiting for one piece and one piece only: the I.

Get these facts into your head first.
So is flat the answer? No: with too little bumpiness, there is nowhere to put a Z or an S cleanly.

And if the bumps are too fine, you will struggle to find a home for pieces like the O.

Lay pieces flat

A stack that is too bumpy causes trouble far more often than one that is too flat, so as a rule aim for a gently contoured shape. Standing pieces upright tends to create steps.
If you find that you somehow always end up with lots of deep wells, practice using pieces laid flat wherever you can.
Placements that lay an S or a Z flat are especially important.
Examples of what looked like a clean stack that accepted plenty of pieces suddenly acquiring a needless spot that waits for an I.
Don’t get too hung up on the idea of “filling in the lowest places first”.

Read NEXT by color and turn your stacking into patterns

Once you are a little more used to the game, you will be stacking while looking at NEXT and thinking about where the next piece should go. When you do, get into the habit of identifying NEXT by its color.
That way you can take in NEXT with it merely sitting in the corner of your vision. In TGM, where gravity keeps on rising, you will never make it in time if you only start thinking about where to put a piece once it has appeared in the playfield.
・As soon as NEXT is displayed, compare it with the stack and decide where it goes
　↓
・Work out in advance the inputs that will get it there, too
　↓
・When the piece appears, carry them out instantly and place it quickly
Unless you can do this, you will not keep up.

A Z can be stacked on a Z, and an S on an S. You will do this constantly when filling two-wide gaps.
When you drop a green (Z) in, you want to register “this now waits for green” without even looking at the shape.

The T is a handy piece that can be dropped into a spot waiting for a Z or one waiting for an S.
When you do that, the wait flips between Z and S.
You can likewise stack a J on a J, or an L on an L.
In this case it is the I that flips the wait.

Read the flow of the piece sequence

In the TGM series the piece sequence is tuned so that pieces arrive in a well-balanced mix.
You rarely get the same piece several times in a row, or a particular piece that just never comes.
So you gain an edge if you can choose placements based on predictions such as “I haven’t had a yellow (O) lately, so one must be due”, even when it is not yet showing in NEXT.

→
A “three-wide gap with a bump in the middle of its floor” is something that comes up quite often.
When a T arrives here, the question is whether to leave a spot that waits for a J or one that waits for an L.
(It is said that after a J has come, an L is three times as likely to come as another J.)
Note also that dropping a Z or an S into this shape leaves you waiting for an I.
Conversely, if an I comes first, putting it in on either side to turn the gap into a two-wide one makes it easy to deal with.
There too you would ideally judge whether to leave it waiting for a Z or for an S, taking into account not just the flow of the piece sequence but also what the other parts of your stack are waiting for.

If the three-wide gap is not yet deep and the step between columns 3 and 4 is two rows, lean a J up against it.
