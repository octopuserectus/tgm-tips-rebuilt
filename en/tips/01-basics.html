Tetris rotation systems fall broadly into two families: “Sega-style” and “BPS-style”. The BPS style has apparently been turning into the “World rule” (SRS), a unified worldwide standard, in recent years.
TGM follows the Sega-style rotation system and, as the table above shows, every piece except the I rotates so that the height of its lowest edge never changes. You could say the pieces roll along the ground.
It may feel a little unnatural at first, but I think this rotation system is what makes twists easy to use and pieces easy to handle at high gravity.

The playfield

The playfield is made up of 20 rows (lines) × 10 columns. Rows are normally counted from the bottom (row 1, row 2, …) and columns from the left (column 1, column 2, …).
As the figure on the left shows, however, a rotated piece is allowed to stick out one row above the top. To rotate an I piece the top three rows of the playfield must be empty, but any other piece can be rotated as long as the top two rows are empty.
The mass of pieces you have stacked up is called the “stack”.
If a piece overlaps the stack as it appears, the game is over; this is called a “top out”.
The upcoming piece shown above the playfield is called the “NEXT display”, or simply “NEXT”.

Levels and maxing out

Progress through the game is expressed as a “level” (Lv).
You start at level 0 and clear the game at level 999. This is called “maxing out” (カンスト, kansuto, short for “counter stop”).
The level goes up by 1 every time a piece appears in the playfield (every piece dealt), and when you clear lines it goes up by the number of lines cleared.
(In other words, maxing out means dropping a little over 700 pieces and clearing a little under 300 lines.)

Each block of 100 levels is called a “section”.
A section boundary (Lv *99 → Lv *00, or Lv 998 → Lv 999 for the last one only) can be crossed only by clearing a line.
Dealt pieces alone leave the level stuck at *99; this is called a “level stop”. In a time attack it is lost time.

Examples: clear 1 line with the piece at Lv 99 and you are at Lv 100; the next piece makes it Lv 101
Clear 4 lines with the piece at Lv 98 and you are at Lv 102; the next piece makes it Lv 103
Clear 4 lines with the piece at Lv 95 and you are at Lv 99; the next piece is also Lv 99

20G

The game shows motion by drawing 60 images a second, and each of those images is called a “frame”.
Gravity, the speed at which pieces fall, is expressed as “how many rows the piece falls per frame”, in a unit called “G”.
One of the most distinctive features of the TGM series, practically its symbol, is 20G, where pieces don’t so much fall as suddenly “appear on the ground”.

Lock delay and ARE

After a piece touches the stack there is a short time before it locks (the piece flashes white for an instant), during which you can still rotate it and move it sideways.
At 20G in particular, where a piece is already on the ground the moment it appears, you use this window to slide the piece along the surface.
This time is called the “lock delay”, and in TGM it is about 0.5 seconds. It gets shorter in TAP’s advanced stages.
Lock delay is reset when the piece drops down a step. (See Chapter 5.)

There is also a short time between a piece locking and the next piece appearing, and this is called “ARE”.
It gets shorter in TAP’s advanced stages.

Manual lock and DAS

Push the stick down while a piece is falling and it falls at high speed. That speed is 1G, so when natural gravity is already faster than that, nothing changes.
Push the stick down while a piece is resting on the ground and the piece is forced to lock even if there is lock delay left.
In the TGM series, the term “manual lock” (下入れ, shita-ire, literally “pressing down”) usually refers to the latter. The former is also called a “soft drop”.

Hold the stick sideways for a while and the piece starts moving sideways at high speed; this is called “DAS”.
(The speed is the equivalent of 1G, so it is the same as entering a sideways input every frame.)
The time it takes to “charge” is called the “DAS delay”, about 0.25 seconds in TGM. It gets shorter in TAP’s advanced stages.
DAS is accepted even before the piece appears, so if you have it already “charged” before then, the piece flies off sideways the instant it appears.
This is important in time attack.

IRS

IRS stands for Initial Rotation System (a system for entering a rotation in advance).
If you are holding a button down when a piece appears, the piece enters the playfield already rotated once.
It gives you comfortable control even when gravity is high, so start using it deliberately early on, until you can do it without thinking.

→
Avoiding a top out with IRS.
Spawned as it is, the piece would top out, but with IRS it can dodge the stack.
When using IRS is what would cause the top out, the button press is ignored and the piece appears as normal. (corrected 2008-08-26)

Wall kicks

→
Consider rotating a piece right next to the wall, as in the figure.
Going by the rotation system introduced above, it looks as if the rotation should fail, because the piece’s left-hand cell would end up inside the wall.
In practice, though, there is room to shift to the right, so the position is corrected automatically and the piece rotates.
This makes it easy to rotate a piece that you have carried all the way to the edge at high speed with DAS, and alongside IRS it is one of the things that make the TGM series so comfortable to control.
It comes in useful extremely often, for example in the twists introduced in Chapter 8 and the movement optimization introduced in Chapter 11.
